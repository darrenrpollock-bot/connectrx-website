Why Generic CRMs Are Failing Australian Medical Device Sales Teams

CE
ConnectRx Editorial
ConnectRx Insights
April 2026
12 min read

For most industries, a CRM is a CRM. You pick Salesforce or HubSpot, configure a few pipelines, train the team, and move on. But medical device sales is not most industries — and the teams that treat it that way are quietly losing ground to those who don't.

The problem isn't that generic CRMs are bad tools. They're excellent tools, built for a broad set of commercial contexts. The problem is that medical device sales has a specific set of requirements that generic platforms were never designed to accommodate — and attempting to bolt on that specificity through customisation, workarounds, and third-party integrations creates systems that are expensive to maintain, slow to adopt, and structurally misaligned with how reps actually work.

"We spent 18 months configuring Salesforce before realising the configuration itself had become the product. Our reps were managing the system instead of managing their territories."

— National Sales Director, Australian orthopaedic device company

What Medical Device Sales Actually Requires

To understand why generic CRMs fall short, it helps to understand what makes medical device sales structurally different from, say, enterprise software or financial services sales.

The HCP Relationship is Multi-Layered

A medical device rep doesn't just manage a single contact at a hospital — they manage a network of relationships that spans surgeons, theatre nurses, procurement teams, clinical educators, and department heads. Each of these relationships sits at a different point in the influence chain for any given device purchase, and each requires different content, different cadence, and different context.

Generic CRMs model this as "contacts" and "accounts." Medical device sales requires something closer to a relationship graph — who influences whom, who controls the budget, who has the clinical authority to champion a new device through a hospital's approval process.

Territory Coverage is a First-Class Metric

In most B2B sales, territory is a loosely defined concept — a region, a vertical, a segment. In medical device sales, territory management is a surgical discipline. Which hospitals have you visited this month? Which surgeons haven't been seen in 90 days? Which theatre lists are you not yet on?

Key finding

In our analysis of field data across ConnectRx customers, reps who log visits within 4 hours of the interaction retain 3× more detail accuracy than those who log at end-of-day — and 7× more than weekly recap loggers.

Generic CRMs have visit logging. They don't have territory intelligence — the ability to surface which parts of your territory are underserved, which relationships are cooling, and which competitive accounts are ripe for a push.

The Configuration Trap

The typical response to these gaps is configuration. Salesforce admins build custom objects for HCP relationship types. Custom fields for theatre access. Custom dashboards for territory coverage. Custom reports for management.

This configuration takes months. It requires ongoing Salesforce expertise — either in-house or via expensive consultants. And critically, it produces a system that looks right in demos but doesn't feel right to reps in the field, because the underlying data model is still a generic CRM trying to impersonate a medical device platform.

  • Adoption suffers when the system doesn't match the mental model of the work
  • Data quality degrades when logging feels like overhead rather than value
  • Management reporting lags because the data coming in is incomplete or inconsistent
  • Compliance risk increases when interaction documentation is unreliable

What the Alternative Looks Like

Purpose-built field intelligence platforms start from the work, not from a generic sales framework. The core data model is built around HCPs, institutions, devices, and interactions — not leads, opportunities, and deals.

Logging a visit takes seconds, not minutes, because the interface is designed for a rep standing outside a theatre. Territory coverage is a live view, not a report you run on Friday. Management dashboards show what's actually happening in the field, not what reps remembered to enter at end-of-week.

The Business Case for Switching

The ROI argument for purpose-built tooling in medical device sales is not primarily a cost argument — it's a revenue argument. Teams with higher-quality field intelligence close more deals, maintain better account coverage, and respond faster to competitive threats.

The configuration and maintenance costs of a generic CRM implementation frequently exceed the cost of a purpose-built platform over a 3-year horizon. But the more compelling number is the revenue impact of better field data — and that's a calculation most companies have never properly run.
